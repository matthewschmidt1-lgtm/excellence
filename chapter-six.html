Chapter six

From Potential to Performance

Northfield already had the answer. It was running in one region, on one person’s say-so. Getting it from there to everywhere turned out to be the whole job.

One region

By now the leadership team agreed. Drivers should replace a broken case on the spot. The receipt had made the argument, the three lenses had picked the answer, and one region had been doing it for four years.

That region was worth a second look. Same company, same trucks, same tea, same pay. Its drivers were no more talented than anyone else’s. Its stores were the happiest Northfield had.

The difference was not the people. It was what surrounded them. One manager had arranged things so that an ordinary driver, on an ordinary morning, could do the right thing without asking. Talent is what people bring. Performance is what the company does with it.

The first try

So the founder stood up at the company meeting and announced it. From Monday, any driver could replace a broken case on the spot. People applauded. It was, after all, what the founder had been saying for nine years.

On Monday a driver in another region found a broken case and wanted to replace it. There were no spare cases on his truck. His handheld had a screen for deliveries and a screen for returns, and nothing for this. He wrote it on a piece of paper, and the store manager filed a claim.

Everybody knew what Northfield wanted. Nobody had been given the means to do it. An announcement moves what people know. It does not move anything else.

The second try

Operations fixed that inside a month. Two spare cases on every truck. One new button on the handheld, which took ten seconds to press. A small budget for each route, and no form.

For three weeks it worked everywhere. Then the monthly report came out, and regional managers saw their replacement costs, which were up, next to their targets, which had not changed. Nobody was told to stop. Managers started asking drivers to “use judgment.” Drivers understood exactly what that meant.

By the end of the quarter, replacements on the spot were back where they had started, in one region.

The third try

The third change was the smallest to describe and the hardest to make. Regional managers stopped being measured on what replacements cost and started being measured on how many stores they kept. The claims team, which now had very few claims, was asked to run the monthly spot check and to find out why bottles were breaking in the first place.

Then came the real test, which is always the first time it goes wrong. A driver on a northern route replaced forty cases in a month, far more than anyone else. Under the old rule that would have meant a meeting and a new approval. This time someone rode along. The cases were breaking on one bad stretch of road, because of how they were stacked. The stacking changed. Nobody added a rule.

Word of that traveled faster than any announcement had. People learn what a company means from what happens next.

Three things, all at once

Northfield needed three things to be true at the same time, and it found them one at a time, the expensive way.

People have to be aligned. That does not mean they agree, or that they have read the strategy. It means a driver alone at a loading dock at six in the morning can make the call the company would want, without phoning anyone. Agreement is a feeling in a meeting. Alignment is what people do when the meeting is not there.

People have to be enabled. They need the means: the tools, the time, the authority, the spare cases on the truck. Most companies skip this one without noticing. They hire good people, tell them what excellent looks like, and surround them with a system that makes it hard.

And the behavior has to be reinforced. What gets measured, praised, promoted and forgiven has to point the same way as what was announced. Where the two disagree, people believe the consequences. They are right to.

Take away any one, and the other two do not add up to two thirds of a result. They add up to very little, as the first two tries showed.

Agreement is a feeling in a meeting. Alignment is what people do when the meeting is not there.

More than the sum

What changed at Northfield is easy to miss. No one became more talented. Nobody was replaced. The same drivers, the same claims team and the same managers produced a different result, because of how their work now fit together.

That is what I mean by collective performance, and it is the multiplier from the last chapter at work. It is not individual performance added up. A company is not as good as its people. It is as good as what happens between them.

It runs both ways. For six years Northfield’s people had been combining into something slower and more cautious than any of them was alone. Nobody chose that either.

Which leaves something awkward for the people at the top. The founder had wanted this for nine years, and said so every year, and it had not happened. Wanting it was not the missing piece. What a leader is actually for, if not that, is the next chapter.

Something to ponder

Somewhere in your company, people have been told to do something they have not been given the means to do.

They know. They are waiting to see whether anyone else notices.
