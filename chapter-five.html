Chapter five

The Three Lenses

Three sensible people looked at the same receipt and saw three different problems. All of them were right.

Three proposals

Ines’s receipt did what the photographs had not. It got a meeting of its own, and this time it was the first item.

Within a week there were three proposals on the table. Each came from someone good at their job, and each one would have worked, in the sense its author meant.

The head of finance looked at a hundred and forty-four thousand dollars and proposed cutting the claims team from four people to two.

The head of operations looked at ten steps and proposed a claims portal, so stores could file online and the form would never be incomplete.

The head of HR looked at the phones and proposed training in customer empathy, with an award each quarter for the best-handled claim.

Finance saw a cost. Operations saw a process. HR saw people. Nobody was wrong about what they saw.

What each one missed

Run them forward. Cut the team in half and the same ten steps are done by two people. Sixteen days becomes thirty. The receipt gets shorter and the wait gets longer.

Build the portal and Northfield has made it faster to do something a store never wanted to do. A form you can file at midnight is still a form. The company would have spent a year improving a process whose best version was not existing.

Train the phone staff and they will apologize more warmly for a decision they are still not allowed to make. People who are asked to care more, inside a system that gives them no button, burn out or leave.

Each proposal improved the thing its author was looking at, and made something worse that its author was not.

Three places to look

I use three lenses when I look at a company. I tend to use them in this order, though the order matters less than using all three.

The first is the business. How it creates value, and for whom. Through this lens you are looking for what the customer came for, and whether the company’s effort is pointed at it. Its blind spot is improving a number without asking what the number is for.

The second is the system. How work flows, how decisions get made, how effort turns into results. A good system makes the right thing the easy thing. Its blind spot is perfecting a process nobody should be running.

The third is people. The judgment, knowledge and care that turn the other two into something real. Its blind spot is expecting people to make up, indefinitely, for a system that makes good work hard.

None of this is complicated. The difficulty is that almost everyone is trained in one lens and promoted for being good at it. A company’s org chart is, more or less, its three lenses kept in separate rooms.

Where they overlap

Now put the regional manager from chapter three back in the picture. Her drivers replaced a broken case on the spot, out of a small budget, and she checked a sample of replacements every month.

Look at it through all three. For the business, the store got what it came for the same day, at the cost of forty dollars and a spot check. For the system, the decision sat with the person standing in front of the broken case, and there was nothing to route, approve or chase. For the people, a driver was trusted to use their judgment, and found that it was worth having.

It was not a compromise between three interests. It was one design that all three lenses agreed on. That is rarer than it sounds, and it is what I mean by a good answer.

The overlaps have names

Where the business meets people, there is leadership. A direction only matters when it changes what people actually do. Where people meet the system, there is capability. What people know becomes performance when the conditions let them use it. Where the system meets the business, there is scale. Results can be repeated without repeating the same problems.

And in the middle, where all three hold, something happens that none of them produces alone. The business defines the value. The system makes it repeatable. People bring it to life. Each makes the other two stronger. I call that the multiplier, and the rest of this book is about building it.

The problems that matter most live where the lenses overlap.

Look three times

There is a habit that follows from this, and it is the most useful thing in the chapter. Before a change goes ahead, look at it three times. Once for what it does for the customer. Once for what it does to the way work flows. Once for what it asks of the people doing it. A change that is good in one and harmful in another is not finished yet.

Northfield’s three proposals each failed that test, and each would have been approved on its own. The driver’s version passed it, and was already running in one region with no approval at all. What it lacked was a way to become how Northfield worked, without depending on one manager. That is the next chapter.

Something to ponder

The last change your company made was probably good for one of these three.

It is worth knowing which one, and who paid for it in the other two.
