Chapter four

The Hidden Cost of Complexity

Every rule is bought once and paid for every day after. Nobody sends the bill.

So she counted

Ines had been told, politely, that her finding was interesting. She had not been told what would happen next. So she did the one thing an analyst can always do. She counted.

She started with a single claim. One case of iced tea, forty dollars, broken in the back of a truck. The approval rule had started at a hundred dollars, and by year nine it covered everything. She followed it through Northfield and wrote down every minute anyone spent on it, and what that minute cost.

Then she did the same for a claim in year one, from the stories people still told about it.

Seventy-two dollars

In year one, replacing a forty-dollar case cost Northfield forty-two dollars. In year nine it cost a hundred and twelve. Northfield was spending seventy-two dollars to decide whether to spend forty.

The number had never been seen, because it did not live anywhere. Six dollars sat in the call center’s budget. Twelve fifty sat with the claims team. The manager’s six minutes sat inside a salary. Each piece was small, reasonable and somebody else’s. Costs that are spread thin enough stop looking like costs. They look like people doing their jobs.

Then she multiplied it out. About two thousand claims a year, at seventy-two dollars each, is a hundred and forty-four thousand dollars a year. The rule had been written to stop false claims. Ines went back through three years of records and estimated, generously, that it stopped about four thousand dollars of them a year.

Northfield was spending a hundred and forty-four thousand dollars a year to save four.

Costs that are spread thin enough stop looking like costs.

Below the tear

That was the part she could count. The bottom of the receipt is the part that never gets printed.

The store manager’s forty-five minutes belong to the store, so Northfield never sees them. The sixteen days with a gap on the shelf show up, eventually, as slightly lower sales, with nothing to say why. The broken case that is never reported does not show up at all. And the shelf that goes to someone else arrives in a quarterly review as “competitive pressure.”

This is why complexity is so hard to argue with. Its benefits are specific and its costs are everywhere. The rule can point to the fraud it stopped. The cost cannot point to anything.

What it does to people

There is one more cost, and it is the one I mind most. The people on the phones at Northfield were hired because they were good with customers. By year nine the job was moving a form from one stage to the next. Nobody had taken their judgment away. There was simply nowhere left to use it.

People adapt to that. They stop noticing the things the system has no box for. The best of them leave, and the exit interview says “limited growth.”

It feeds itself

Complexity also grows on its own. The form was sometimes filled in wrong, so there was a checklist for the form. Claims sometimes stalled, so there was a status report. The status report took time, so there was a coordinator. Each addition fixed a problem the last addition had made.

At some point a company crosses a line without noticing. More of its effort goes into running itself than into serving the customer. It is busy, it is skilled, and it has become very good at managing the consequences of its own decisions.

Activity is not progress. A company can work harder every year and deliver less.

What the number was for

Ines did not bring this to a meeting as an accusation. The rule had been reasonable. The people running it were doing it well. What she brought was a receipt, and a receipt does not blame anyone. It only says what was paid.

That mattered, because of the forces in the last chapter. A number with nobody’s name on it was safe to say out loud. It put the customer back in the room. And it gave the people who were measured on replacement costs a much larger cost to be measured against.

It did not fix anything. A cost you can see is only a cost you can see. But for the first time in six years the rule had to be worth what it cost, and somebody had to say so. How to look at a company so that this becomes normal is the next chapter.

Something to ponder

Somewhere in your company there is a rule that costs more than the thing it prevents.

Nobody is hiding the number. Nobody has been asked to add it up.
