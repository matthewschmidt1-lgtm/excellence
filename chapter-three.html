Chapter three

The Seven Forces

Ines had seen the problem. What happened next had almost nothing to do with her, and everything to do with the company around her.

What happened to the photographs

Ines put her findings in front of her manager on a Tuesday. Eleven unclaimed cases. Fourteen stores. One sentence from a store manager, written down word for word.

Nothing dramatic happened. Nobody shouted and nobody buried it. Over the next six weeks it was received, appreciated, discussed, scheduled and, in the end, not acted on.

I have watched that happen to good findings for twenty years, and for a long time I blamed the finding. Not clear enough, not enough data, wrong slide order. It is rarely the finding. A company’s response to the truth is decided by a small number of forces that were in place long before the truth showed up.

I count seven. Here is each one, at Northfield.

  1. 01

    Awareness.

    Seeing what is actually happening.

    This was the one Northfield now had, because one person got on a truck. It is where everything starts, and on its own it changes nothing.

  2. 02

    Trust.

    Making it safe to say so.

    Ines’s manager read the store manager’s sentence twice and then asked, kindly, whether it needed to be in the deck. The last person to question the 91 percent had been asked to take it offline. It never came back online.

  3. 03

    Conditions.

    Making good work possible.

    The people on the phones wanted to help the store. Their screen had no button for it. Without an approved form the system would not issue a replacement, so willingness had nowhere to go.

  4. 04

    Value.

    Keeping the customer in the room.

    When the finding reached the leadership meeting it was the ninth item of nine. The first eight were about budgets, headcount and a reorganization. The customer is not in the meeting until someone brings them in, and item nine got four minutes.

  5. 05

    Incentives.

    Rewarding what you say you want.

    Northfield said fix it today. It paid the claims team for closing claims within ten days and the approving manager for keeping replacement costs down. People heard both messages and believed the one with money attached.

  6. 06

    Leverage.

    Making an improvement last without a hero.

    One regional manager had quietly solved all of this. Her drivers replaced broken cases on the spot, out of a budget she defended every quarter. Her stores were the happiest in the company. It worked because of her, which means it stopped when she went on vacation.

  7. 07

    Excellence.

    Holding the standard when nobody is checking.

    The standard had once been today. Nobody could say when it became ten days, and nobody was holding either number against what a store needed. A standard that only moves in one direction is not a standard. It is a record of what the company has put up with.

They multiply

It is tempting to read that as a list of seven problems. It is one problem. The forces are not separate. Each one sets the ceiling for the others.

Seeing, without trust, becomes a secret. Without conditions it becomes frustration. Without value it becomes activity. Without incentives it becomes a contradiction. Without leverage it becomes one person’s heroics. Without excellence it becomes a thing everyone knows and nobody expects to change.

That is why strength in one place does not make up for absence in another. Northfield had excellent awareness by the end of the last chapter. It got the result of its weakest force, not its strongest.

The one holding the rest

Look at what is beyond the line. Northfield was not short of talent, or data, or people who cared. It had all of that and could not use it. Most of what a struggling company needs, it already has. It is stranded above the level of its weakest force.

This is not my idea. Eliyahu Goldratt built a whole way of running factories on it: find the one constraint, and work on that. Polishing what is already strong feels like progress and changes nothing downstream.

It also explains something that puzzled me for years. The same finding, put to two companies, produces two different outcomes. Nothing about the finding is different. What is different is everything around it.

A company gets the result of its weakest force, not its strongest.

Whichever way they point

The forces are also not still. They are things a company does, every day, and they compound in whichever direction they point.

When a person tells the truth and it goes badly, trust drops, so the next person says less, so awareness drops, so the standard slips a little further. Nobody chose that. It ran downhill by itself.

It runs the other way just as reliably. Someone says an awkward thing and is thanked. The next awkward thing arrives sooner. A fix gets built into the system, so it holds, so people believe the next fix is worth proposing. That is all culture is. It is what everyday decisions and consequences reinforce.

So the useful thing to know about a company is not how strong each force is today. It is which one is holding the rest, and which way it is moving.

Northfield’s was incentives. It said one thing and paid for another, and until that changed, nothing Ines found could travel very far. Before anyone would change it, someone had to be able to say what it was costing. That is the next chapter.

Something to ponder

Somewhere in your company there is a person quietly making something work that the system does not.

Notice who it is. Then notice what happens when they take a week off.
