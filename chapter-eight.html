Chapter eight

The Pursuit of Excellence

Northfield did not become a company that stops drifting. There is no such company. It became one that notices sooner and comes back.

Year twelve

Three years later, Northfield was a good company to deal with again. Stores said so. The spare cases rode on every truck, the button took ten seconds, and the claims team had become four people who knew more about why bottles break than anyone in the business.

It would be a tidy place to stop. It would also be chapter one. Northfield had felt finished before, in its first year, and what followed was nine years of sensible decisions.

So here is what happened next, because it is the part that matters.

The same morning, again

In the spring of year twelve, the monthly spot check turned up a driver who had replaced thirty cases at one store in three weeks. It was not a bad road this time. The store belonged to his brother-in-law.

It cost about twelve hundred dollars, which is twice what the two false claims had cost in year three.

And the room did what rooms do. Someone proposed that any replacement of five cases or more should need a manager’s approval. Nobody objected at first, because it was a sensible answer to a real problem.

You have read this chapter. It was the first one.

What was different

Three things were different this time, and none of them was that the people were wiser.

The first was how long it took to find. In year three nobody was looking, and the pattern came to light by accident. In year twelve the spot check found it in three weeks, because looking was somebody’s job.

The second was that someone could say so. A regional manager said, out loud, that this was how the last one started. Nine years earlier that sentence would have been taken offline.

The third was the card. Every rule at Northfield now had to say what it protected against and what it would cost. So before the rule was written, Ines’s old team priced it. Five hundred approvals a year, to stop a problem that had happened once. The driver was dealt with, as a person who had done something wrong. The rule was never written.

That is all that happened. It took one meeting. I think it is the most impressive thing in this book.

The habit of coming back

I used to think of excellence as a level a company reaches. I don’t anymore. Drift is the default, as chapter one said, and it does not stop being the default because a company had a good year.

Excellence is not the absence of drift. It is the habit of coming back. A company wanders from its customer a little every day, the way attention wanders, and the excellent ones are not the ones that never wander. They are the ones that notice sooner, say so without fuss, and return.

That is why the measure I trust most is a distance. Not how few mistakes a company makes, but how long it takes between finding one and changing whatever produced it. Northfield’s first bad rule lasted six years. Its second lasted one meeting.

Excellence is not the absence of drift. It is the habit of coming back.

Three kinds of better

There are three kinds of better, and they are easy to confuse. A company can do things right, which is efficiency. The claims portal would have been that. It can do the right things, which is effectiveness. Letting the driver decide was that. Or it can become a company that keeps finding the right things and keeps getting better at doing them, without waiting for a crisis or a hero. Only the third one lasts, because only the third improves the thing that does the improving.

It is also the quiet one. Efficiency shows up in a report. Effectiveness shows up in a result. The third kind shows up as a meeting where a bad rule does not get written, and nothing happens, and nobody outside the room ever knows. Steve Jobs’s father taught him to finish the back of a cabinet, the part nobody sees. This is the back of the cabinet.

Three sentences

The whole of this book fits in the three sentences on its cover.

See clearly. Go and look, especially at what everyone has learned to accept, and especially from where the customer stands.

Understand deeply. Find the forces underneath, look through all three lenses, and work out what the thing costs and who is paying.

Act intentionally. Change the system that produced the problem, put the decision where the work is, and make the better way the easier way, so that it lasts without a hero.

Then do it again, because it does not stay done. Preserve the purpose. Challenge the assumptions. Change the system.

Six o’clock

In the fall of year twelve a new analyst joined Northfield and, in her first month, asked why drivers were allowed to give away product without approval.

Nobody gave her the history. Ines handed her the card, which said what the rule was for, what it cost, and when it would next be looked at. Then she told her which truck left at six.

Excellence is the operating system that makes it last.

Something to ponder

Whatever you fix will begin to drift the day after you fix it.

That is not a failure. Notice sooner, and come back. Even a small return is a real one.
