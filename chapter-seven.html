Chapter seven

Leadership as System Design

The founder had wanted the right thing for nine years. It turned out that wanting was never the job.

The fastest claims process at Northfield

For nine years the founder did two things about broken cases. Once a year, at the company meeting, came the line everyone knew: “we fix it today.” And a few times a month, when a store was angry enough to find the right number, the founder took the call personally and had a case on a truck within the hour.

Both felt like leadership. The first was a speech. The second was a rescue.

The speech changed nothing, for the reasons in the last chapter. The rescues were worse than nothing, and it took a long time to see why. Every one of them worked. Every one of them also taught Northfield that the way to get a store looked after was to reach the founder.

By year nine, the fastest claims process the company had was the founder’s cell phone.

A leader who fixes the case leaves the system that produced it exactly as it was, and adds one thing to it. The company now needs them.

What a leader can actually move

Look at what the founder did in the third try, the one that worked. It was not a speech. It was a change to what regional managers were measured on, and nobody else in the company could have made it.

That is the pattern. A leader has surprisingly few real tools, and a speech is not one of them. There is where a decision sits. There is what gets measured and paid for. There is what the leader asks about on an ordinary Tuesday. There is what happens to the first person who gets it wrong. And there is what gets taken away.

None of those is doing the work. All of them shape the work everyone else does. That is what I mean by design. The founder’s job was never to replace the case. It was to build a company where the case got replaced.

The job was never to replace the case. It was to build a company where the case got replaced.

When to step in

This does not mean a leader should never step in. It means knowing which kind of problem is in front of you.

Step in when a decision cannot be undone. Step in when the truth has stopped traveling, and people are telling you what is calm instead of what is so. Step in when a problem sits between two departments and belongs to neither, because nobody below you can pick it up.

For nearly everything else, the better move is slower and less satisfying. Leave the case to the people closest to it, and go and change whatever made it hard for them. The rescue feels like leadership. The repair is leadership.

Rules that carry their reasons

There is a risk in all of this, and Northfield nearly walked into it. Once a company sees what one bad rule cost, it wants to tear out the rest.

G. K. Chesterton had a line about fences. Do not take one down until you know why it was put up. It cuts both ways. A rule nobody can explain should not be removed on a hunch. It should not be kept on one either.

So the founder asked for something simple. Every rule at Northfield got a card with four lines on it: what it protects against, what it costs, who owns it, and when it will next be looked at.

They went through forty rules that year. Thirty-one stayed exactly as they were, because they were holding something up and now everyone could say what. Six were changed. Three were removed. Nobody remembers the three.

That is the difference between governing and controlling. A governed company can tell you what each rule is for. A controlled one can only tell you that it is the rule.

What stands when you are not there

The following summer the founder took three weeks off, properly, for the first time since the company began.

A case broke on a Tuesday, in a region the founder had never visited. The driver replaced it and pressed the button. The store manager said thank you. Nobody above the truck heard about it until the monthly spot check, where it was one line among two hundred.

The founder’s phone did not ring. It was the best result of the year.

I think that is the fairest measure of a leader. Not how much the company needs them, but how much it can do because of them when they are somewhere else. Nobody judges a building by its foundation. It is standing on one all the same.

Northfield was now, by most measures, fixed. That is the dangerous moment, because fixed is how it felt in year one as well. The last chapter is about what it takes to stay that way.

Something to ponder

Somewhere in your company, a decision keeps traveling upward because nobody closer to it is allowed to make it.

Someone could go on answering it well. Or answer it once, by deciding who should.
